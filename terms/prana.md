# prāṇa

English candidates: **intake; inward vital wind**.

## Root and derivation

The prefix/root analysis proposed for the vital winds has **not yet been checked against a grammatical source**. Functional translation is kept separate from derivation.

## Textual evidence

### Suśruta Saṃhitā, Nidānasthāna 1.13

Status: **checked against the Sanskrit e-text**, not collated against a manuscript or printed scan. [Text](https://github.com/sarit/SARIT-corpus/blob/1eac9ee0b055c8d11147edac4a75c76008ccc363/susrutasamhita.xml).

```text
yo vāyurvaktrasaṃcārī sa prāṇo nāma dehadhṛk/ so+annaṃ praveśayatyantaḥ prāṇāṃścāpyavalambate//
```

Literal English: Wind moving in the mouth is named prāṇa, sustaining the body; it causes food to enter within and supports the vital breaths.

### Caraka Saṃhitā, Cikitsāsthāna 28.6

Status: **checked against the Sanskrit e-text**, not collated against a manuscript or printed scan. [Text](https://github.com/sarit/SARIT-corpus/blob/1eac9ee0b055c8d11147edac4a75c76008ccc363/carakasamhita.xml).

Literal English of the passage: The seats of prāṇa are the head, chest, throat, tongue, mouth and nose. Its actions include spitting, sneezing, eructation, breathing and taking food.

Comparison: Caraka gives several seats and actions, broader than the mouth-and-food emphasis in Suśruta 1.13. “Intake” remains a selective functional label, not a complete definition.

## Reading of the proposed English

Intake captures entry of food, but omits its sustaining functions. The proposal’s restriction to the living’s words is a design choice. SARIT represents elided initial vowels by `+`; that editorial notation is retained. Anusvāra glyph ṁ is normalized to ṃ.

## Further grammatical check

**Checked root:** Dhātupāṭha 2.64–65 reads `śvasa prāṇane / ana ca` ([text](https://sanskritdocuments.org/doc_z_misc_major_works/dhatupatha.html)); the “and” carries the preceding sense, breathing, to an. Thus pra + √an has a checked root basis. The noun affix and retroflexion have not yet been fully traced through the grammar. “Breathing forth” is a morphological gloss; the medical subtype also admits food, as the quoted Suśruta passage shows.
